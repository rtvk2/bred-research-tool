What the study was about

We were studying false recognition: people sometimes say they have seen a word before when they have not, especially when it looks like a word they did see. Some words in the list were made-up words, made by changing one letter of a real word (for example BANARA, from BANANA). The real words that looked like them (for example BANANA) were never in the list. We wanted to know whether information about what other people said changes how often people wrongly say OLD to such look-alike words, and whether it matters if that information says OLD or NEW and how many people agree.

What was not as described, and why

The answers of the “other participants” (for example “9 of 10 other participants said OLD”) were not real. The researchers produced them following a fixed pattern that has nothing to do with whether a word was really in the list, and no earlier participants gave them. We did not tell you at the start because knowing this would have changed how you responded. We are sorry that we could not tell you earlier. For some words no such information was shown, so that we can compare those words with the others.

If you said OLD to a word that was not in the list

That is a very common and normal memory error. It does not say anything about how good your memory is. We are interested in how often it happens in different situations across many people, not in how any one person did.

What we expect

We expect that people will say OLD to look-alike words more often when the information says other people said OLD, and less often when it says NEW, and that the effect may be stronger when more people agree. We will not know until all the data have been collected and analysed.
